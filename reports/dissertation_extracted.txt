[Heading 1] Chapter 1: Introduction
[Heading 2] 1.1 Introduction
[Normal] Food prices affect whether people can afford enough food and whether they can maintain a reasonable diet. When prices rise quickly, households with low or irregular incomes are usually affected first. They may buy less food, change the type of food they buy, borrow money, or reduce spending on other needs. Food price changes are therefore not only an economic issue. They are also connected to livelihoods, health, agriculture, and social welfare. Zimbabwe is a suitable case for studying this problem because food prices are affected by rainfall, agricultural production, currency conditions, transport costs, and differences between local markets. These conditions can change at the same time, making it difficult to identify where food price pressure is likely to appear next. This project uses data science methods to combine market price records and rainfall information in order to predict possible food price stress at district level. The chapter introduces the project, explains its background and the current state of knowledge, identifies the gap being addressed, and presents the problem statement, aim, objectives, motivation, stakeholders, scope, and assumptions.
[Heading 2] 1.2 Background and Context of the Project
[Normal] Zimbabwe depends heavily on rain fed agriculture. This means that poor rainfall can affect crop production, household income, livestock, food availability, and prices. The effect is not always immediate. Traders and consumers may change their expectations during the growing season, before the final harvest position is known. If a rainfall deficit is followed by low market supply, transport difficulties, or a shortage of foreign currency for imports, food prices may rise further. 
[Normal] The country also has a complicated food price environment because different currencies and pricing conditions have been used over time. Market records may be expressed in Zimbabwean dollars or United States dollars, and the meaning of a nominal price changes when the currency loses value or when the exchange-rate system changes. Comparing raw prices across all periods can therefore give a misleading picture. A price may rise because of general inflation, currency conversion, higher transport costs, or a real shortage in a particular market. Food prices also differ between districts. A market near a surplus-producing area may receive replacement supplies more easily than a remote market in a dry region. Roads, fuel prices, distance, border procedures, and trader networks affect how quickly food moves from one place to another. For this reason, a national average may hide price stress in a particular district or commodity group. This project uses data from the World Food Programme food price monitoring system, CHIRPS rainfall estimates, and administrative boundary information. These sources are joined into a monthly dataset in which market observations can be linked to districts and local rainfall conditions. The project then uses price movement, rainfall anomalies, seasonal patterns, peer-market differences, commodity categories, and currency information as inputs to a predictive model.
[Heading 3] 1.2.1 Current State of Knowledge
[Normal] Existing research shows that food prices can change because of production shocks, storage conditions, trade costs, currency movements, and demand. Storage can reduce normal price variation, but the buffer becomes less effective when stocks are low or when a shortage is expected . Prices can therefore move sharply instead of changing in a smooth and predictable way. Research on Zimbabwe has also examined food insecurity, household vulnerability, drought, agricultural production, and market integration. Studies of Zimbabwean households show that income, labour, livestock, remittances, access to information, and social protection influence food access. Other studies show that rainfall and temperature affect agricultural production and that the impact differs between dryland and irrigated farming areas. Research on Zimbabwean markets also suggests that price links between districts are not always strong and that trade policies can change the source of imported food.
[Normal] Food security organizations already collect and analyse useful information. WFP monitors prices and markets, FEWS NET produces food  security outlooks, and ZimVAC and ZimLAC assess household livelihoods and vulnerability. These systems are important because they combine data with expert knowledge. Remote-sensing datasets such as CHIRPS also make it possible to monitor rainfall in areas where ground weather stations are limited  . Recent data science research has used machine learning to combine climate, vegetation, soil moisture, prices, and other information for food security forecasting. Tree based models are useful for this type of data because they can represent non-linear relationships and interactions. However, the model must be evaluated in a way that respects the order of time. Randomly mixing earlier and later observations can make a forecasting model appear more accurate than it would be when used on new data.
[Heading 3] 1.2.2 Gap Identification
[Normal] Although Zimbabwe has food price monitoring, climate information, and household vulnerability assessments, these sources are not always combined into one district level system that produces a forward looking food price stress signal. Existing reports are valuable, but they may describe conditions that have already developed or use different reporting periods and geographic units. This makes it difficult to compare a rainfall condition in one district with a price condition in the same district and month. It also makes it difficult to produce one repeatable output that can be updated when a new month of market data becomes available. There is also a data science gap. A model can give misleading results if prices are compared across different currency conditions without adjustment, if markets are assigned to the wrong district, or if future values enter the feature table. The same problem can occur when data are randomly split even though the aim is to predict a later month. A suitable project must therefore deal with data quality, spatial matching, time order, class imbalance and model interpretation as part of the full process. The gap addressed by this project is therefore practical, technical, and analytical. Practically there is a need for a market stress warning that can support earlier checking and planning. Technically there is a need to join price, rainfall and administrative data in a consistent way. Analytically there is a need to test whether climate and market variables can be used together without data leakage. This project addresses the gap through a monthly market commodity dataset, engineered price and rainfall features, time ordered model evaluation and a scoring process that can be used on new uploads.
[Heading 2] 1.3 Problem Statement
[Normal] Food price stress in Zimbabwe can develop through the combined effects of rainfall deficits, agricultural losses, currency changes, transport costs, and local market conditions. However, it is difficult to identify the timing and location of the next price stress event from separate market and climate reports. A national price average may also hide important differences between districts and commodities. The main problem addressed by this project is the lack of an integrated and reproducible data science system for predicting next month food price stress at subnational level. The problem also includes the risk of using future information by mistake when creating features or validating a model. A useful system must therefore combine the available data, use only information that would have been available at the time of prediction, and produce an output that can support further investigation and planning.
[Heading 2] 1.4 Project Aim
[Normal] The aim of this project is to develop and evaluate a machine learning system that uses food price data, rainfall anomalies and market information to predict possible food price stress in Zimbabwean districts in the following month. The aim has two connected parts. The first part is to build a reliable data process. This involves bringing together the price, rainfall, geographic, commodity, currency and population information, checking the quality of the data and creating features that represent the conditions present before the prediction month. The second part is to develop a useful prediction process. This involves training suitable classification models, comparing them with simple baselines, testing them on later observations and producing probabilities that can be reviewed by users. The project is not aimed at predicting every part of food insecurity or replacing existing national assessments. It focuses on a clearly defined market outcome: whether a market commodity observation is likely to meet the project’s food price stress condition in the following month. The final system is intended to support early investigation and decision making. It is not intended to make automatic decisions about households, districts or assistance.
[Heading 2] 1.5 Project Objectives
[Normal] The project has five objectives:
[List Paragraph] To combine WFP food price records, CHIRPS rainfall estimates, market locations and administrative boundaries into a consistent monthly dataset.
[List Paragraph] To create useful price, rainfall, seasonal, spatial, commodity and currency features for predicting next month food price stress.
[List Paragraph] To compare selected machine learning models with simple baseline models using time-ordered validation.
[List Paragraph] To develop a scoring process that checks new data, applies the trained model, and produces forward-looking risk probabilities without changing the original historical data.
[List Paragraph] To present the predictions by commodity and district in a form that can support market monitoring and early warning decisions.
[Heading 2] 1.6 Motivation of the Project
[Normal] The project is motivated by the need for earlier information about food market pressure in Zimbabwe. When a price increase is only noticed after it has already affected households, government departments and humanitarian organizations have less time to respond. A model that uses current market information and rainfall conditions may help identify areas that require closer checking before the situation becomes worse. The project is also motivated by an interest in applying data science to a real problem in Zimbabwe. The data come from different sources and are not immediately ready for analysis. They have to be cleaned, linked, checked, and arranged in time order before a model can be trained. This makes the project relevant to both data science and informatics. It involves data integration, feature engineering, machine learning, evaluation, visualisation and the design of a practical information system. Another reason for undertaking the project is that a model should be tested honestly. It is not enough to obtain a high score from a random split of the data. The model must be tested on later observations, because that is how it will be used in practice. The project therefore gives attention to time order, leakage prevention, baseline comparison and the limitations of the available data.

[Heading 2] 1.8 Project Scope
[Normal] The project covers food price stress in Zimbabwe at market, commodity and administrative district level. It uses monthly food price observations and links them to district level rainfall conditions and market information. The main commodity groups represented in the project data are cereals and tubers, pulses and nuts, oils and fats, meat, fish and eggs, and miscellaneous food items.  The project includes data cleaning, administrative harmonisation, feature engineering, target construction, machine learning model development, time-ordered validation, model comparison, probability scoring and geospatial visualisation. It also includes a prospective scoring process for new market price uploads. The project does not predict household food consumption, poverty, malnutrition, famine, or individual vulnerability. It does not provide a long-term national food security forecast. The main prediction concerns whether the defined food price stress condition is likely to occur in the following month. The climate data are limited mainly to CHIRPS rainfall and derived measures. Temperature, soil moisture, vegetation condition, crop yield, conflict, fuel prices, border closures, informal exchange rates and household survey data are not part of the main feature set. These factors may still affect food prices, so the results should be interpreted within the limits of the available data.

[Heading 2] 2.6 Key Concepts and Themes
[Normal] The themes below connect the economic, social, environmental, and data science literature. Together, they explain why food price stress should be studied as an interaction between market conditions, climate signals, spatial relationships, and household purchasing power rather than as a simple price series .
[Heading 3] 2.6.1 Food price stress and household food access
[Normal] Food price stress refers in this project to an unusual or concerning increase in the price of a food commodity in a market. It is not the same as household food insecurity. A household may experience food insecurity because of unemployment, illness, lack of land, poor harvests, or loss of income even when prices are stable. Similarly, a household with income, savings, livestock, remittances, or food production may manage a price increase better than a household without those resources . Tawodzera (2011) shows that urban households in Epworth experienced food insecurity through unemployment, poverty, high dependency, and rising food prices. The study is important because it demonstrates that urban households can be highly exposed to food price changes when they depend on purchased food and unstable incomes. Mango et al. (2014) provide evidence from Mudzi that labour, education, livestock, remittances, and market information influence household food security. These factors may change the effect of a market price shock on different households.
[Normal]   examine cash transfers and household vulnerability in Zimbabwe. Their findings show that social protection can improve some food security outcomes, but they also show that food security and diet diversity are not measured in exactly the same way.   connect livestock market participation with food expenditure and dietary diversity in Gwanda and Mangwe. These studies indicate that market participation can improve purchasing capacity, but the benefits are not equally available to all households. The literature supports using food prices as an early warning input while setting a clear boundary for the project. The project can identify a market price signal, but it cannot classify the food insecurity of an individual household without household-level data. A market prediction should therefore be combined with livelihood and vulnerability assessments rather than presented as a complete food security result .
[Heading 3] 2.6.2 Currency, inflation, and food prices in Zimbabwe
[Normal] Zimbabwe’s monetary history affects how food prices should be measured and interpreted. Kramarenko et al. (2010) describe the policy challenges after hyperinflation and show the connection between monetary conditions, fiscal pressures, external competitiveness, and confidence. The World Bank (2021) links exchange rate depreciation, inflation, and economic deterioration to food price increases and losses in household purchasing power.   adds a political economy perspective by arguing that Zimbabwe’s food crisis during 2019–2020 was shaped by economic decline, climate conditions, COVID-19 disruptions, income losses, and weaknesses in social protection. These studies show that food price movement cannot be explained only by agricultural production. Prices may also react to currency shortages, import costs, exchange-rate expectations, and the ability of households to obtain income. 
[Normal] The use of more than one currency creates a measurement problem. A nominal increase in local-currency terms may reflect depreciation rather than a comparable change in the underlying market relationship. Prices in United States dollars may also change for different reasons from prices recorded in local currency. Kabundi et al. (2022) show more broadly that food prices in Sub-Saharan Africa are affected by international prices, exchange rates, domestic supply conditions, and policy factors. This literature supports the use of log returns, standardised price movement, peer-market comparisons, and currency indicators. It also supports preserving the original currency context instead of converting every record into one nominal series without considering the exchange-rate information that would be added or lost .
[Heading 3] 2.6.3 Storage, seasonality and non-linear price behaviour
[Normal] Agricultural supply is seasonal and uncertain, while demand for staple foods continues throughout the year. Storage allows traders, households, governments, and other market participants to move supply across time. Williams and Wright (1991) explain that storage decisions depend on expected future prices, holding costs, spoilage, financing, risk, and the availability of trade.   show how competitive storage can produce non-linear price behaviour. When stocks are available, a supply shock may be absorbed and prices may move only moderately. When stocks are depleted, the buffer disappears and the same shock may produce a large price increase. This helps explain why food price stress may appear suddenly rather than increase in a smooth and proportional way.   provide empirical evidence that inventory conditions are related to commodity-price volatility. Their exchange-based data are different from Zimbabwean retail markets, so the result should not be transferred directly. However, the study supports the general idea that market buffers affect the intensity of price movement.
[Normal] Gilbert et al. (2017) show that food price seasonality differs across African markets and commodities. This is important because a normal seasonal increase should not automatically be labelled as stress. A useful project must distinguish predictable seasonal movement from unusual acceleration associated with drought, trade restrictions, currency instability, or low market stocks. The storage and seasonality literature supports the use of lagged prices, rolling returns, seasonal variables, and standardised stress rules. It also supports allowing non-linear relationships in the model rather than assuming that every additional unit of rainfall or price movement has the same effect  
[Heading 3] 2.6.4 Spatial markets and price transmission
[Normal] Spatial market analysis examines how prices in different locations are connected through trade.   explain that price differences can exist because of transport and transaction costs, but arbitrage and trade may transmit shocks between markets. Market integration does not mean that prices are always equal. It means that price relationships respond to economic connections over time. Zimbabwe-specific research supports this view. Mahdi and Bonato (2015) identify differences between northern grain-surplus markets and southern deficit markets and report stronger links along some trade corridors than between all provinces. This suggests that Zimbabwe should not be represented as one uniform market. Location, commodity, road connections, and trade routes matter.   show that cross-border trade controls change price transmission between Zimbabwe, Zambia, and South Africa. When one supply route is restricted, another source may become more important. Burke and Myers (2014) provide related evidence from Southern African maize markets connected through informal trade, showing that trade volumes and transfer costs affect the strength and speed of transmission.
[Normal] Porteous (2019) estimates the consequences of high trade costs for African agricultural storage and trade. The study is useful because it shows why annual averages can miss the timing of stock movement and trade decisions. Monthly data can capture changes that would disappear in an annual series. This literature supports administrative harmonization, peer-market features, district level analysis, and the comparison of markets selling the same commodity. It also warns against interpreting a spatial difference as evidence of market failure without considering transport costs, product quality, demand, trade policy, and currency conditions  .
[Heading 3] 2.6.5 Climate variability and agricultural production
[Normal] Zimbabwean agriculture is strongly affected by rainfall variability.  examine the connection between ENSO, seasonal rainfall, and simulated maize yields in Zimbabwe. Their findings show that climate conditions can influence yield variability, but the effect differs by season and location.  use a Ricardian approach to examine the economic effects of climate change on Zimbabwean agriculture and show that the effect depends on local environmental and production conditions. The 2023/24 El Niño event demonstrates the importance of anticipatory climate information in Southern Africa.  discuss the likely effects on agriculture and the need for early action in Zimbabwe.   use Earth-observation data to assess ENSO effects on African agriculture, while Shukla et al. (2020) show how operational hydrological monitoring can improve early warning of drought-driven food insecurity.
[Normal] CHIRPS is relevant to this project because it provides a long rainfall record at a useful spatial and temporal resolution. Funk et al. (2015) describe CHIRPS as a satellite and station-based precipitation dataset designed for monitoring rainfall extremes and climate hazards. Its use can help provide information in areas where weather-station coverage is limited. The climate literature supports district levelrainfall anomalies, lagged rainfall features, and cumulative rainfall measures. It also sets a clear limitation. Rainfall is not the same as soil moisture, vegetation condition, crop yield, or household food access. Temperature, irrigation, crop type, soil, water storage, and local market access may change the effect of the same rainfall anomaly .
[Heading 3] 2.6.6 Existing monitoring and early warning systems
[Normal] Zimbabwe already has organizations that collect and interpret food, livelihood, market, and climate information. WFP market analysis considers prices, availability, supply chains, macroeconomic conditions, policy, and access. FEWS NET produces food security outlooks using scenario development, evidence, and expert analysis. ZimVAC and ZimLAC provide national and subnational livelihood and vulnerability assessments ( . The main strength of these systems is that they combine quantitative information with local knowledge and expert interpretation. Their reports can explain why a market signal matters and how it relates to household conditions. They also provide information that is not available in the project’s price and rainfall files, such as livelihood strategies, coping behaviour, food consumption, and assistance coverage. The limitation for this project is not that existing systems are inadequate. Rather, they do not all provide the same machine-readable, monthly, district–commodity probability of next month price stress. Their reporting cycles, geographic units, targets, and methods differ. The proposed project therefore aims to complement rather than replace them. Its output should be checked against the existing monitoring systems before it is used for planning .
[Heading 2] 2.7 Synthesis of Findings
[Normal] The literature shows several areas of agreement. First, food price stress is influenced by more than agricultural production. Storage, trade costs, currency, expectations, imports, transport, and market access also affect the final price observed by households. Second, climate shocks do not affect all districts equally. Production systems, market connections, water availability, and trade routes influence whether a rainfall deficit becomes a local price problem  . Third, household food insecurity is broader than market price stress. Income, assets, labour, remittances, livestock, own production, social protection, and access to information affect whether a household can respond to higher prices  . This supports treating the project’s output as a market early warning signal rather than as a direct household classification.There are also differences between the studies. Household studies measure access, welfare, and diet, while market studies measure prices and transmission. Climate studies often focus on rainfall, production, or drought, while monitoring systems may use food security categories and expert judgement. Machine learning studies use different targets, time periods, geographic scales, and evaluation designs. Results from one group cannot be transferred directly to another (Westerveld et al., 2021; Busker et al., 2024).
[Normal] A further theme is the importance of time. Price and climate variables are serially related, and a future observation can easily enter a feature through an unshifted rolling average, group statistic, imputation value, or random split. Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) explain why ordinary cross-validation may be inappropriate for time-series predictor evaluation. He et al. (2021) also highlight changing relationships and limited observations as challenges in climate forecasting. The literature therefore supports a project that uses chronological validation, separates the target from the predictor matrix, compares machine learning models with persistence and majority-class baselines, and reports more than accuracy. Precision–recall measures are useful when stress events are uncommon, while calibration measures whether a probability can be interpreted as a useful risk estimate (Busker et al., 2024; Westerveld et al., 2021). The main gap is that the reviewed Zimbabwean literature does not provide one integrated, reproducible, district–commodity system that combines market prices, rainfall anomalies, currency context, peer-market features, time-respecting validation, and prospective scoring. The global literature provides useful methods, but its targets and settings differ from Zimbabwe. This project addresses the gap by adapting established ideas to Zimbabwean data and by documenting the complete process from data integration to decision-support output.
[Heading 2] 2.8 Methodological Approaches 
[Normal] The reviewed literature uses several methodological approaches. Econometric studies use regression, instrumental variables, spatial price models, time-series analysis, and dynamic models. These methods are useful when the purpose is to estimate associations, investigate market transmission, or study the relationship between a specific explanatory variable and an outcome. They can also be easier to interpret than complex machine learning methods (Fackler & Goodwin, 2001; Melesse et al., 2023). Storage models and market-equilibrium approaches explain how supply, demand, inventories, trade costs, and expectations may affect prices. Their strength is that they provide an economic explanation for non-linear behaviour. Their limitation is that they may require information about stocks, trade, or expectations that is not available in the project dataset (Deaton & Laroque, 1992; Porteous, 2019). Climate and remote-sensing studies use rainfall records, CHIRPS, vegetation measures, soil moisture, hydrological products, and crop simulations. These approaches are useful when ground observations are limited and when the aim is to monitor drought or production conditions. Their limitation is that environmental stress does not automatically equal a market price event or household food insecurity (Funk et al., 2015; Sazib et al., 2020).
[Normal] Machine learning studies use logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting, neural networks, and other classification or regression methods. Westerveld et al. (2021) use transferable features to forecast changes in food security conditions. Busker et al. (2024) apply machine learning to food security crisis forecasting in the Horn of Africa. Reichstein et al. (2019) argue that data-driven Earth-system research should remain connected to process understanding rather than becoming an unexplained automated procedure. The literature also identifies important weaknesses. Complex models may be difficult to interpret, small datasets can lead to unstable results, and random validation can produce over-optimistic estimates when the task is forecasting. A model may also be affected by changes in the relationship between climate, markets, and the target over time (He et al., 2021; Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012). For this project, a small set of models is more suitable than using many algorithms without a clear purpose. Logistic regression provides an interpretable benchmark. Tree-based gradient boosting can represent non-linear patterns and interactions. Persistence and majority-class rules provide practical baselines. The models are evaluated using time-ordered splits, several performance measures, and a later holdout period. This approach follows the literature’s emphasis on matching the method to the prediction problem. The project uses CRISP-DM to organise the full data science process. CRISP-DM is appropriate because the work includes project understanding, data understanding, preparation, modelling, evaluation, and a prospective scoring stage. The methodology is not selected because it guarantees a good model. It is selected because it provides a structured way to document each stage and to identify problems before the final prediction is produced.
[Heading 2] 2.9 Relevant Technologies and Tools
[Normal] The project uses Python as the main programming language because it can combine data preparation, spatial processing, machine learning, visualisation, and deployment within one workflow. Previous data science and Earth-system studies have used programming-based workflows to combine environmental and socio-economic data, while Reichstein et al. (2019) emphasise that data-driven methods should remain connected to the process being studied. The project uses pandas and NumPy for cleaning the WFP price records, aggregating observations by month, creating lagged and rolling variables, and performing numerical calculations. This is preferred to manual matching because geographic assignment can be repeated and checked. CHIRPS is used to provide rainfall information because Funk et al. The project uses scikit-learn for logistic regression, histogram-based gradient boosting, time-ordered data splitting, preprocessing, and evaluation metrics. XGBoost is included as a comparison model because tree-boosting methods can represent non-linear relationships and interactions that are relevant to climate and food price data. Similar machine learning approaches have been applied to food security forecasting by Westerveld et al. (2021) and Busker et al. (2024), although their study settings and targets differ from this Zimbabwean project. Optuna is used to tune the tree-based model because it can search several interacting parameters more efficiently than testing every combination manually.

These tools were selected instead of using only spreadsheets because the complete process needs to be repeatable and connected to the modelling code. [Heading 2] 2.10 Comparison with Existing Work
[Normal] The project builds on existing Zimbabwean work in several ways. Like Mahdi and Bonato (2015), it recognises that food prices differ between locations and that market integration is affected by trade routes. Like Davids et al. (2017) and Burke and Myers (2014), it recognises that trade policy, informal trade, and transfer costs can change price transmission. Like climate studies by Phillips et al. (1998) and Mugiyo et al. (2023), it treats rainfall and ENSO-related conditions as important early signals rather than waiting only for final production figures. The project also follows existing food security monitoring practice by treating the model as decision support rather than as a replacement for expert assessment. WFP, FEWS NET, ZimVAC, and ZimLAC use different types of evidence and have different purposes. The project adds a predictive market signal that can be compared with these sources, but it does not attempt to reproduce household assessments or expert food security classifications (World Food Programme, 2025; Zimbabwe Vulnerability Assessment Committee, 2022). The project differs from many earlier studies because it combines market prices, rainfall anomalies, spatial information, commodity categories, currency context, peer-market measures, and a forward target in one monthly prediction task. It does not only describe household food insecurity, estimate one price relationship, or map drought. It joins several types of information and produces a probability of a predefined next month market stress condition.
[Normal] The project also differs in its treatment of validation. The target is defined for the following month, future fields are excluded from the feature matrix, and expanding-window validation is used to imitate future deployment. The model is compared with persistence because price stress may continue from one month to the next. These choices respond to concerns about time-series validation raised by Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) and to the changing conditions described by He et al. (2021). The project is not completely new in its use of machine learning, rainfall, or price data. Its contribution is the adaptation and integration of established ideas for a Zimbabwean market problem. The gap is not the absence of all previous research. It is the lack of one documented and reproducible process that brings the relevant data and validation requirements together at market, commodity, and district level. The main limitations of existing work also guide the project. Zimbabwean studies provide detailed context but often focus on households, production, or individual market relationships. Global machine learning studies provide useful forecasting approaches but may use different targets, locations, and data structures. The project therefore avoids claiming that one method from the literature is automatically appropriate. Instead, it uses local context to define the problem and uses comparative literature to select and test suitable methods.

The reviewed work shows that food prices are influenced by climate, storage, seasonality, currency, trade, market connectivity, and household purchasing power. [Heading 2] 3.1 Introduction
[Normal] This chapter explains how the project was carried out to predict possible food price stress in Zimbabwe. It describes the research methodology, research design, data sources, data preparation, feature engineering, target construction, model selection, training, tuning, validation, statistical analysis, software, and ethical considerations. The structure follows the Level 4.2 Data Science and Informatics project guidelines and the CRISP-DM approach used to organise the work  . The project uses historical food price and rainfall data to estimate whether a defined market–commodity stress condition may occur in the following month. This requires more than fitting a machine learning algorithm. The data must be joined at the correct geographic level, dates must be placed in the correct order, future information must be excluded, and the final output must be understandable to the intended users. These requirements are consistent with literature showing that forecasting models can be misleading when time order, changing relationships, and validation design are not considered . The chapter describes the methods only. The descriptive results, model performance, feature importance, and district findings are presented in Chapter 4.
[Heading 2] 3.2 Research Methodology
[Normal] The project follows the CRISP-DM methodology, which means Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining. CRISP-DM provides stages for understanding the problem, understanding the data, preparing the data, developing models, evaluating the models, and deploying the final solution .
[Normal] The stages were adapted to the project as follows:
[List Paragraph] Project understanding: Define food price stress, identify stakeholders, and establish the purpose of the early warning system.
[List Paragraph] Data understanding: Inspect the WFP price records, CHIRPS rainfall data, market locations, administrative boundaries, population information, and project files.
[List Paragraph] Data preparation: Clean the records, standardise names and dates, integrate the datasets, create the target, and construct the predictors.
[List Paragraph] Modelling: Train and compare logistic regression, gradient-boosting, XGBoost, persistence, and majority-class models.
[List Paragraph] Evaluation: Use time-ordered validation, a forward holdout, classification measures, probability measures, and leakage checks.
[List Paragraph] Deployment: Save the selected model and use the prospective scoring and mapping scripts to process new data.
[Normal] CRISP-DM was selected because this project includes both analytical and informatics tasks. It is necessary to understand the food market problem, process several data sources, develop a model, and prepare an updateable application. The methodology also supports iteration. If a geographic join, target definition, or validation procedure is found to be unsuitable, the relevant stage can be reviewed before final reporting .
[Heading 2] 3.3 Research Design
[Normal] The project uses a quantitative, retrospective, longitudinal, and predictive research design. It is quantitative because it analyses numerical prices, rainfall measures, population information, and model probabilities. It is retrospective because it uses historical observations. It is longitudinal because the observations are ordered by month. It is predictive because the main outcome concerns the following month rather than only describing the current period. The design is not experimental. Rainfall, prices, currency, trade, and market access are not controlled by the researcher. The project therefore does not claim that the fitted relationships prove that rainfall causes a particular price movement. The variables are used to identify patterns that may support early warning. This distinction is important because climate and market studies often identify associations without establishing a complete causal mechanism .
[Normal] The unit of analysis is a market–commodity–month observation linked to an administrative district. This unit preserves differences between local markets and commodities instead of reducing all observations to a national monthly average. It also allows prices to be compared with eligible peer markets selling the same commodity under a similar currency context. Spatial market research supports this approach because trade costs and market connections can differ between locations (Fackler & Goodwin, 2001; Mahdi & Bonato, 2015). The design follows four rules. First, the target refers to the month after the predictor month. Second, predictors are created only from information available at or before the forecast date. Third, training and testing follow calendar order. Fourth, the output is a market early warning signal and not a direct classification of household food insecurity, which depends on income, assets, production, and social protection as well as prices (Mango et al., 2014; Bhalla et al., 2018).
[Heading 2] 3.4 Data Collection and Preprocessing
[Normal] Data collection and preprocessing establish the foundation for the modelling stage. The project uses secondary data from market monitoring, rainfall monitoring, administrative geography, and district population records. Combining these sources requires attention to time resolution, geographic identifiers, currencies, missing data, and differences in measurement .
[Heading 3] 3.4.1 Data Sources and Acquisition
[Normal] The main price file is the World Food Programme food price dataset stored in the project as food_prices_zwe_core.parquet. It contains the recorded date or period, market, commodity, commodity group, unit, price type, currency, and nominal price. The file provides the market information used to construct the monthly price panel. Rainfall information comes from the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with Station dataset, known as CHIRPS. The project uses the processed district-month file adm2_monthly_rainfall.parquet. CHIRPS combines satellite information with station observations and was designed to support monitoring of rainfall variation and extremes (Funk et al., 2015). The geographic files include market_admin_crosswalk.csv and the Zimbabwe administrative boundary file zwe_adm2.geojson. District population information is used as contextual data after the market has been linked to an administrative district. The project also uses local scripts, tables, figures, and model files stored in the project folder. The project uses secondary data and does not collect information directly from households  .

[Heading 3] 3.4.3 Data Quality Assessment
[Normal] Data quality was assessed before model fitting. The checks covered missing dates, prices, currencies, market names, commodity labels, coordinates, districts, rainfall values, and peer-market observations. Duplicate records were identified, and prices were checked for zero, negative, or otherwise unusable values before logarithmic calculations were performed. Market and commodity names were examined for spelling differences and inconsistent labels. Geographic coordinates were checked for valid ranges, and each market was reviewed to determine whether it could be assigned to one district. The rainfall file was checked for the required districts and months. The final panel was then sorted by the identifiers needed for time-based feature construction. The quality assessment did not aim to remove every extreme value. A sharp price increase or a severe rainfall anomaly may be a genuine event that the project is intended to identify. The purpose was to separate likely data errors from unusual but meaningful observations. This distinction is important in climate and commodity analysis because extreme observations may contain the most important information .
[Heading 3] 3.4.4 Data Cleaning and Preprocessing
[Normal] Dates were converted into a consistent monthly representation. Market names, commodity names, currency labels, and administrative identifiers were standardized before the datasets were joined. Invalid price values were removed from calculations requiring logarithms, while observations were not discarded solely because they were large or unusual.
[Normal] When more than one valid price existed for the same market, commodity, currency, and month, the monthly median was used as the representative value. The median reduces the influence of one unusually high or low entry. This is appropriate where individual market records may contain reporting variation and where the objective is to construct a stable monthly signal rather than preserve every transaction entry. Missing values were handled according to their role. A missing future price was not treated as a negative stress outcome. Rows without a valid future label were excluded from supervised training and evaluation. Any imputation, scaling, or standardization used during modelling was fitted within the training period so that information from the validation period was not transferred backwards. The data were sorted by market, commodity, currency, district, and date before lags and rolling features were calculated. This step ensures that a lag represents an earlier observation and not simply a different row in the dataset. Time-series studies warn that ordinary random validation can produce misleading results when observations are ordered and related over time  .
[Heading 3] 3.4.5 Data Integration and Merging
[Normal] The price, rainfall, geographic, and population files were joined using dates, market identifiers, commodity information, currency, and administrative geography. Where a reliable district identifier existed, it was standardised and used. Where it was missing, the market coordinates were linked to administrative polygons through a point-in-polygon join. The geographic join used the WGS84 coordinate reference system and GeoPandas. Markets outside the boundary file or assigned ambiguously were flagged rather than being placed in a district without evidence. This approach follows the principle that spatial integration should be reproducible and should preserve the uncertainty of the geographic assignment  . Rainfall was joined by district and month. Markets in the same district therefore receive the same district rainfall value for a given month, while price variables retain market and commodity differences. Population information was merged by district and used as a contextual feature, not as a direct measure of household food security.
[Heading 3] 3.4.6 Feature Engineering
[Normal] Feature engineering was based on the mechanisms identified in the literature and the project objectives. Current log return, lagged returns, and rolling returns represent price momentum and persistence. These features are supported by storage theory, which explains why current prices may reflect expectations about future supply and stock conditions . Peer market features compare a market with eligible markets selling the same commodity in the same currency context. The peer median and the deviation from that median help distinguish a broad price movement from a local disturbance. This follows spatial market research showing that price transmission depends on trade costs, corridors, and market integration . Rainfall was transformed into a district and calendar-month anomaly. A lagged rainfall anomaly represents a delayed effect, while a rolling rainfall sum represents accumulated conditions. This approach is supported by Zimbabwean and African climate research showing that rainfall and ENSO effects may operate through agricultural and market processes over time rather than only in the same month  .

[Heading 3] 3.4.7 Dimensionality Reduction
[Normal] A formal dimensionality reduction procedure such as principal component analysis was not used as the main approach. The feature set was kept relatively small and was selected from the research questions, project objectives, and literature. Keeping the original variables makes it easier to explain how rainfall, price movement, currency, and market differences contribute to the prediction. Feature review, correlation checks, removal of future-information fields, and model regularisation were used to control unnecessary complexity. Principal component analysis could reduce correlation, but it would also combine meaningful features into components that may be difficult to interpret for district level early warning. This decision follows the broader recommendation that data-driven environmental models should retain a connection to the processes represented by the variables .
[Heading 3] 3.4.8 Data Sampling
[Normal] The project does not use random sampling as the main approach because the data form a time-ordered monitoring panel. Random sampling could place later observations in the training data and remove the forecasting sequence. Instead, earlier observations are used for training and later observations for validation and testing. The data are evaluated with chronological windows. Stress cases are less common than non-stress cases, so the project does not rely on accuracy alone. It uses ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, recall, precision, balanced accuracy, calibration, and Brier loss. The use of several measures is consistent with early warning research where missing a positive event and producing a false alarm have different implications . Synthetic oversampling was not used in the main pipeline. If oversampling is tested in future work, it should be carried out only inside each training window. Applying it before the split could allow information from the validation period to influence the training process.
[Heading 3] 3.4.9 Data Privacy and Anonymisation
[Normal] The project uses market, climate, geographic, and population data rather than personal household records. No names, identity numbers, telephone numbers, individual addresses, or personal financial records are included in the modelling table. Market identifiers and coordinates are used for geographic analysis, but the outputs are presented as market, commodity, or district patterns. They are not used to identify individual traders or households. Files are stored in the project workspace and sensitive credentials are not included in the GitHub repository or Streamlit application. District levelpredictions should not be presented as individual household assessments because vulnerability also depends on income, assets, production, labour, and social protection (Mango et al., 2014; Bhalla et al., 2018).
[Heading 3] 3.4.10 Data Documentation
[Normal] A data dictionary records the field name, meaning, data type, source, transformation, and use in the model. The project also records the target definition, feature list, join keys, date range, excluded fields, and validation rules. Documentation distinguishes a price return calculated from a monthly median from a raw daily price, and a district rainfall anomaly from a national rainfall total. Recording these definitions supports reproducibility in data-driven environmental research (Reichstein et al., 2019).
[Heading 3] 3.4.11 Data Versioning and Storage
[Normal] Raw files are kept separate from cleaned files, feature files, model outputs, prediction files, figures, and application files. The prospective scoring process does not overwrite the historical archive. New uploads are checked and saved as separate outputs. Git is used to record changes to scripts, documentation, configuration, and application files. GitHub provides the remote repository and connects the Streamlit application to the versioned project. Raw data containing restricted information and secret credentials are excluded from the repository. This separation improves traceability and allows an earlier working version to be restored when required, supporting the reproducibility expected in data driven environmental analysis (Reichstein et al., 2019).

[Heading 2] 3.5 Model Selection
[Normal] The project compares models with different levels of complexity. Elastic-net logistic regression provides an interpretable benchmark and is suitable when predictors such as current and lagged returns are correlated. Regularization reduces the effect of unnecessary coefficients and supports a more stable model. Histogram-based gradient boosting is used because tree-based methods can represent non-linear relationships, thresholds, and interactions. XGBoost is included as a second boosting method with regularization and shrinkage. These choices follow food security forecasting research that uses machine learning to combine climate, environmental, and socio-economic features .
[Normal] A persistence baseline assumes that the recent market condition provides information about the following month. This is important because food price stress may continue over several months. A majority-class baseline predicts the most common class and shows what can be achieved without learning a useful pattern. Baselines are necessary because a complex model should demonstrate value beyond a simple rule . The project does not use a large number of algorithms simply to search for the highest score. Neural networks, support vector machines, and additional boosting libraries would require more tuning and could make interpretation more difficult without directly addressing the project objectives. The selected models provide a balance between interpretability, non-linear modelling, and practical deployment (He et al., 2021; Reichstein et al., 2019).
[Heading 2] 3.6 Model Training and Evaluation

Each model is fitted inside the training period, and the validation period is used only for evaluation. This design prevents later outcomes from influencing earlier model fitting and follows recommendations for evaluating time-series predictors (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012).
[Normal] The evaluation measures are:
[List Paragraph] ROC-AUC: Measures the ability to rank stress observations above non-stress observations across thresholds.
[List Paragraph] PR-AUC: Focuses on the positive stress class and is useful when stress events are less common.
[List Paragraph] Balanced accuracy: Gives equal importance to sensitivity and specificity.
[List Paragraph] Precision: Measures the proportion of predicted stress observations that are correct.
[List Paragraph] Recall: Measures the proportion of actual stress observations that are identified.
[List Paragraph] F1-score: Combines precision and recall.
[List Paragraph] Brier loss: Measures the difference between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.
[List Paragraph] Confusion matrix: Shows true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives at a selected threshold.
[Normal] The project reports several measures because a model can rank cases well without producing reliable probabilities. It can also achieve good overall accuracy while missing a large proportion of stress events. This is why early warning studies commonly examine discrimination, positive-class performance, and calibration together .
[Heading 2] 3.7 Model Tuning and Optimisation
[Normal] The histogram based gradient boosting model is tuned with Optuna using a Tree structured Parzen Estimator search. The search space includes learning rate, maximum iterations, maximum leaf nodes, minimum samples per leaf  and L2 regularization. The tuning procedure is performed inside the time-ordered validation design. Preprocessing values, feature transformations, and validation scores are calculated without using the final future holdout. This is necessary because using the holdout during tuning would make the final evaluation optimistic (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012). The selected search values favour gradual learning, controlled tree complexity, sufficient observations in each leaf, and regularisation. These choices are suitable for a panel containing correlated lagged variables and relatively uncommon stress events. Optuna was selected instead of a large grid search because it can use information from earlier trials to focus later trials on promising combinations. The tuning result is treated as a configuration selected under the specified validation design, not as proof that the model is permanently optimal (He et al., 2021).
[Heading 2] 3.8 Model Validation and Testing
[Normal] The main validation procedure is expanding-window validation. In the first split, the model is trained on the earliest available history and evaluated on the next period. In later splits, the training window expands to include more historical observations before the next later validation period is scored. Expanding-window validation is more suitable than random $k$-fold cross-validation because the project predicts later observations. Random splitting can place future patterns in the training set and can produce an unrealistically favourable estimate when prices and climate variables are serially correlated (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012). A separate forward holdout period is retained for the final prospective-style check. Model selection and tuning are completed without using the holdout outcomes. The holdout is intended to show whether the model remains useful under later market, climate, currency, or reporting conditions. The difference between validation and holdout performance is treated as useful information about generalisation rather than hidden. Leakage checks are applied to future price fields, future rainfall fields, target labels, and features calculated from later records. Rolling features, group statistics, peer-market measures, scaling, and imputation are checked using truncated historical data. Earlier feature values should not change when later records are added to the archive. This procedure follows the literature’s warning that forecasting systems can appear accurate when future information enters the predictor set (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012; He et al., 2021).
[Heading 2] 3.9 Statistical Analysis
[Normal] Descriptive analysis is used before model fitting to understand the data. It includes summary statistics, class proportions, distributions, missing-value counts, correlation checks, and plots of price and rainfall variables. These analyses help identify skewness, unusual values, imbalance, and possible errors before the modelling stage (Gilbert et al., 2017). Cross-correlation is used to examine the timing of the relationship between rainfall anomalies and price returns. This helps determine whether rainfall should be represented contemporaneously, with lags, or through cumulative measures. The analysis is descriptive and does not establish causality because prices may also be affected by currency, trade, stocks, policy, and demand (Phillips et al., 1998; Kabundi et al., 2022).
[Normal] Permutation importance is calculated on data not used to fit the model. One feature is shuffled while the other features remain unchanged, and the change in the evaluation measure is recorded. This indicates how much the fitted model depends on a feature in that evaluation sample. It does not prove that the feature causes food price stress, particularly where predictors are correlated (Reichstein et al., 2019). The project uses distributions, rainfall–price plots, ROC and precision–recall curves, calibration plots, confusion matrices, feature-importance charts, commodity comparisons, and district risk maps. The visualisations are used to explain the data and the fitted model; they do not replace numerical evaluation or local interpretation.
[Heading 2] 3.10 Software and Tools
[Normal] Python is the main programming language because it supports one connected workflow from data processing to model deployment. pandas and NumPy are used for tabular and numerical operations, GeoPandas for spatial joins, scikit-learn for preprocessing and evaluation, XGBoost for comparative boosting, Optuna for tuning, and Matplotlib and Seaborn for visualisation. CHIRPS supplies the rainfall information used to construct district levelclimate features (Funk et al., 2015; Reichstein et al., 2019). The project uses live_prediction.py to check new files, reconstruct features, and generate probabilities. map_stress.py converts scored records into district leveloutputs. Git and GitHub provide version control, while Streamlit and Streamlit Cloud provide the interactive application and hosting. These tools were selected because they are compatible, accessible, and suitable for the project scale. Alternatives could be used, but the Python workflow reduces complexity. Model files are stored using joblib or pickle, and outputs are checked through file, column, date, row-count, and visual checks because software cannot decide whether a geographic join or target definition is conceptually correct (Reichstein et al., 2019).
[Heading 2] 3.11 Ethical Considerations
[Normal] The project uses secondary market, climate, geographic, and population data and does not collect personal information from households. Privacy risk is therefore limited, but the project avoids presenting district levelpredictions as individual household assessments. This boundary is necessary because household food insecurity depends on livelihood conditions that are not represented fully by market prices (Mango et al., 2014; Bhalla et al., 2018). There is a risk of misinterpretation. A high-risk prediction may be caused by a data problem, a reporting gap, a currency change, or an unusual market condition. It should not automatically be interpreted as proof that all households in a district require assistance. Human verification is required before important decisions are made, consistent with the role of existing food security monitoring systems (World Food Programme, 2025; Zimbabwe Livelihoods Assessment Committee, 2024). The project also considers fairness between districts. Areas with fewer markets or missing records may receive less reliable predictions, so missing information should remain visible. The model supports officials, local knowledge, household assessments, and humanitarian judgement rather than replacing them.
[Heading 2] 3.12 Chapter Summary
[Normal] This chapter described the methodology used to develop the food price stress prediction project. CRISP-DM was selected to organise the work from project understanding through deployment. The project uses a quantitative, longitudinal, and predictive design based on monthly market–commodity observations, following the need for time-aware validation identified in the literature (Chapman et al., 2000; Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012). The data sources include WFP food prices, CHIRPS rainfall, administrative boundaries, market coordinates, and district population information. The data are cleaned, joined, checked, and transformed into price, peer-market, climate, seasonal, interaction, commodity, currency, and contextual features. The target is defined for the following month and future information is excluded from the predictor set (Funk et al., 2015; Fackler & Goodwin, 2001).
[Normal] Elastic-net logistic regression, histogram-based gradient boosting, XGBoost, persistence, and majority-class baselines are compared. Expanding-window validation, a forward holdout, leakage checks, classification measures, probability measures, and feature analysis are used to evaluate the models. The project also includes a prospective scoring pipeline, GitHub version control, Streamlit Cloud hosting, and district levelmapping. Chapter 4 presents the descriptive analysis, model performance, visualisations, and findings produced using these methods. The methodology does not predetermine the results; it provides a transparent procedure for producing and evaluating them.

[Heading 2] 4.1 Introduction
[Normal] This chapter presents the results produced from the food price stress prediction project. It reports the prepared data, climate and price patterns, model performance, feature importance, commodity differences, and district levelrisk outputs. The chapter follows the five project objectives introduced in Chapter 1 and uses the methodology described in Chapter 3. The findings are interpreted in relation to the literature on food prices, storage, spatial markets, climate variability, and machine learning early warning. The results concern market price stress and should not be interpreted as direct measurements of household food insecurity. Household food access also depends on income, assets, labour, remittances, own production, and social protection (Tawodzera, 2011; Mango et al., 2014; Bhalla et al., 2018). The project contains two related target definitions. The first identifies an unusually large standardised price movement in the following month. The second identifies a market price that is high compared with eligible peer markets using the Standardised Price Index (SPI). These targets are related but are not identical. Results using the two targets are therefore identified separately.
[Heading 2] 4.2 Summary of Key Findings
[Normal] The findings are organised around the five project objectives and interpreted using the market, climate, and early warning literature reviewed in Chapter 2 (Shukla et al., 2020; Westerveld et al., 2021).
[Heading 3] 4.2.1 Objective 1: Data integration and administrative harmonisation
[Normal] The first objective was to combine WFP food prices, CHIRPS rainfall, market locations, administrative boundaries, and district information into a usable monthly dataset. The project created a market–commodity panel by standardising dates and labels, aggregating valid prices by month, matching markets to administrative districts, and joining rainfall by district and month. The geographic join was important because not every price record contained the same level of administrative detail. Records with reliable district identifiers were standardised, while records without clear identifiers were assigned using market coordinates and district polygons. Uncertain assignments were flagged rather than silently allocated. This approach is consistent with spatial-market research showing that local market relationships and geographic connectivity should not be reduced to one national average (Fackler & Goodwin, 2001; Mahdi & Bonato, 2015). The resulting files retain market, district, P-code, commodity, currency, and date fields. This makes it possible to trace a model input back to the market and month from which it came. The first objective was therefore met at the level required for the project.
[Heading 3] 4.2.2 Objective 2: Climate and price feature construction
[Normal] The second objective was to create price, rainfall, seasonal, peer-market, currency, and interaction features. The recorded analysis showed a negative relationship between rainfall anomalies and later price returns. The strongest reported association occurred at a two-month lead, with r = -0.22 and a reported significance level below 0.001. The three-month lead also showed a negative association of approximately -0.15. The climate evidence is shown in Figure 4.1. The left panel reports the two lead-period associations available in the evidence. The right panel reports the fitted interaction scenarios: predicted stress increased from approximately 0.12 under normal rainfall and low price momentum to 0.78 when a dry condition occurred with positive price momentum. These are reported model scenarios, not a new simulation of the raw data.
[Normal] Fig 4.2.2: Climate Evidence: timing and combined stress conditions
[Normal] The findings support the inclusion of lagged rainfall and a climate–price interaction. They are also consistent with literature showing that rainfall and ENSO effects may reach markets through production, supply expectations, stocks, and trade over more than one month (Phillips et al., 1998; Mugiyo et al., 2023; Sazib et al., 2020).
[Heading 3] 4.2.3 Objective 3: Model comparison and evaluation
[Normal] The third objective was to compare machine learning models with simple baselines using time-ordered validation. XGBoost recorded the highest mean cross-validation ROC-AUC for the broader SPI target at 0.789. Elastic-net logistic regression recorded 0.759, while the persistence baseline recorded 0.764. The model comparison is shown in Figure 4.2. Persistence had the highest mean PR-AUC at 0.432, which shows that recent market conditions contain useful information about the positive class. The Optuna histogram-based model recorded the lowest reported Brier loss at 0.088, but it used the stricter standardised log-return target. Its holdout ROC-AUC was 0.419, compared with 0.220 for the persistence holdout. Both holdout values were below 0.500, so the later-period ranking results should be interpreted cautiously.
[Normal] Table 4.2.3: Model discrimination
[Normal] The results support the use of several evaluation metrics. A model with a strong ROC-AUC may not have the strongest PR-AUC, and a model that ranks observations reasonably well may still require calibration before its probabilities are used for decisions. This is consistent with food security forecasting literature that recommends evaluating ranking, positive-class detection, and probability quality together (Westerveld et al., 2021; Busker et al., 2024).
[Heading 3] 4.2.4 Objective 4: Prospective scoring process
[Normal] The fourth objective was to develop a process that can accept new data and generate predictions. The project includes live_prediction.py, which checks the structure of an uploaded file, verifies the date progression, reconstructs the required features, checks peer-market availability, and applies the saved model. The scoring process creates a separate output and does not overwrite the historical archive. The output can be inspected and passed to map_stress.py for district levelvisualisation. This provides a repeatable route from a future upload to a prediction table or map. Reproducible, updateable pipelines are important because environmental and food security relationships can change over time (He et al., 2021; Reichstein et al., 2019).
[Heading 3] 4.2.5 Objective 5: Commodity and district decision support
[Normal] The fifth objective was to present model information by commodity and district. The strongest reported XGBoost commodity result was for oils and fats at 0.871, followed by miscellaneous food at 0.828 and cereals and tubers at 0.795. Persistence performed slightly better than XGBoost for meat, fish and eggs and for pulses and nuts. The project also grouped scored observations into Lower, Watch, and Higher risk bands. The reported distribution was 68.4% Lower, 21.2% Watch, and 10.4% Higher. Beitbridge, Chiredzi, Bikita, and Buhera appeared among the districts with higher predicted risk. These outputs are intended to support further checking and not to replace local assessments, as market connectivity and vulnerability differ across locations (Mahdi & Bonato, 2015; Zimbabwe Vulnerability Assessment Committee, 2022).

Logistic regression identified 427 true stress events and recorded a reported recall of 62.1%, while missing 261 positive observations. Its higher recall means that it identified more reported stress cases, but a model with higher recall can also produce more false alerts. The results show a practical trade-off. XGBoost is more conservative at the reported threshold, while logistic regression is more sensitive. The appropriate threshold depends on whether users are more concerned about unnecessary investigations or missed stress events. Threshold choice should therefore be agreed with users rather than selected only from the highest accuracy.

This indicates lower probability error in that evaluation. Calibration should still be monitored after deployment because exchange-rate changes, droughts, missing data, and reporting changes can alter the relationship between predicted probabilities and later outcomes (He et al., 2021).
[Heading 2] 4.5 Inferential Statistics
[Normal] The inferential results examine the timing and combined interpretation of climate and price signals, while recognising that the observational design does not establish causality (Phillips et al., 1998; Kabundi et al., 2022).
[Heading 3] 4.5.1 Rainfall and price lead lag relationship
[Normal] Figure 4.1 reports the available rainfall–price lead results. The strongest negative association occurred at a two-month lead, with r = -0.22 and a reported significance level below 0.001. The three-month lead was approximately -0.15. The pattern supports the use of lagged rainfall features and suggests that rainfall conditions may affect price expectations before the final production outcome is observed. It is consistent with Zimbabwean research linking ENSO and rainfall conditions to maize-yield variability and with Southern African early warning research using hydrological monitoring (Phillips et al., 1998; Shukla et al., 2020). The correlation is not close to one, so rainfall is not the only explanation for price movement. Currency, imports, stocks, demand, trade policy, and transport may operate at the same time. The result is therefore evidence of association and timing, not proof of a causal rainfall-to-price mechanism (Kabundi et al., 2022).
[Heading 3] 4.5.2 Climate price interaction
[Normal] The interaction is interpreted in the context of climate-sensitive market mechanisms rather than as a causal estimate (He et al., 2021; Reichstein et al., 2019). The right panel of Figure 4.1 reports the fitted interaction scenarios. When price momentum is near zero, a severe rainfall deficit raises the reported predicted stress probability from approximately 0.12 to 0.35. When rainfall is normal and current price return is positive, the reported probability is approximately 0.42. When a dry condition occurs together with positive price momentum, the reported probability reaches approximately 0.78. This pattern indicates that the fitted model treats the two signals as reinforcing one another. The same rainfall anomaly has a different model implication depending on the current price condition. The interaction therefore provides a useful way to communicate compound risk, while remaining a model explanation rather than a causal graph.

[Heading 2] 4.8 Outliers and Anomalies
[Normal] The return variables contain large positive values. These observations represent sharp price increases and may be genuine stress events rather than data errors. They were therefore not automatically removed. The monthly median reduced the influence of isolated reporting entries while preserving genuine price movement. Rainfall anomalies also include dry and wet extremes. A severe rainfall anomaly may represent a real climate event, so it was not deleted only because it was far from the mean. Instead, the project used district and calendar-month standardisation and checked for impossible or missing values. This is consistent with the use of CHIRPS for monitoring climate extremes (Funk et al., 2015). Other anomalies may result from currency changes, market-reporting gaps, commodity relabelling, or changes in market coverage. These conditions can create apparent price jumps that require investigation. The results should therefore be interpreted alongside source metadata and local market information rather than treated as an automatic diagnosis (Kramarenko et al., 2010; World Bank, 2021).
[Heading 2] 4.9 Robustness and Sensitivity Analysis
[Normal] The main robustness check compares expanding-window validation with the forward holdout. Cross-validation performance is more favourable than the later holdout performance, which suggests that the relationship between the predictors and stress may change across periods. This is plausible in a market affected by currency changes, rainfall variation, policy, and changing trade routes (He et al., 2021; Davids et al., 2017). A second check compares the models with persistence and majority-class baselines. Persistence remains competitive, showing that recent market conditions contain useful information. The comparison prevents the project from claiming that a complex model is useful simply because it performs better than a random or uninformative baseline. Commodity results also differ: XGBoost performs strongly for oils and fats, miscellaneous food, and cereals and tubers, while persistence performs slightly better for meat, fish and eggs and pulses and nuts. This reflects differences in seasonality, storage, perishability, and market integration (Gilbert et al., 2017; Porteous, 2019). A fourth check uses several metrics. ROC-AUC describes ranking, PR-AUC focuses on the positive class, balanced accuracy considers both classes, and Brier loss evaluates probabilities. Differences between these measures show that model quality depends on the intended decision use rather than on one score alone (Westerveld et al., 2021; Busker et al., 2024).
[Heading 2] 4.10 Discussion and Interpretation
[Normal] The discussion connects the findings with economic, climate, market, and machine learning literature and considers their practical meaning for early warning (Deaton & Laroque, 1992; Reichstein et al., 2019).
[Heading 3] 4.10.1 Recap of key findings
[Normal] The recap is linked to the project literature on climate signals, market momentum, and interacting supply mechanisms (Williams & Wright, 1991; Phillips et al., 1998). The project successfully combined market prices, rainfall, geographic information, currency, commodity, and district context into a monthly prediction panel. Rainfall anomalies had a negative lead–lag relationship with later price returns. The interaction evidence showed higher predicted stress when dry conditions occurred together with positive price movement. XGBoost produced the strongest cross-validation ROC-AUC for the broader SPI target, while persistence produced the strongest PR-AUC. The Optuna histogram-based model had the lowest reported Brier loss but weaker forward holdout discrimination. Rainfall anomaly was the most important positive feature in the permutation analysis, followed by recent price movement and the climate–price interaction.
[Heading 3] 4.10.2 Interpretation of the results
[Normal] The results suggest that food price stress is not represented by one signal. Rainfall, recent price movement, peer-market conditions, currency, commodity type, and local context provide different information. A rainfall deficit may have a limited market effect where stocks and imports are available, but its effect may be stronger when prices are already increasing and trade options are restricted. This interpretation is consistent with storage and market-transmission theory (Williams & Wright, 1991; Deaton & Laroque, 1992; Fackler & Goodwin, 2001). The lead–lag result supports using rainfall as an early signal, but the relationship is not strong enough to justify a rainfall-only warning. The combined feature set is more appropriate. The forward holdout limits the claims that can be made. A relative improvement over a weak persistence value does not guarantee accurate ranking in every future period, so the system supports monitored decision assistance rather than automatic action.
[Heading 3] 4.10.3 Comparison with prior knowledge
[Normal] The findings agree with storage research showing that prices can respond non-linearly when market buffers are weak. They also agree with African market research showing that seasonality, trade costs, and market integration differ by commodity and location (Deaton & Laroque, 1992; Gilbert et al., 2017; Porteous, 2019). The climate findings are consistent with Zimbabwean and Southern African research linking rainfall variability and ENSO to agricultural outcomes. The project extends this literature by connecting rainfall anomalies to a market price stress task rather than measuring crop yield or household welfare directly (Phillips et al., 1998; Mugiyo et al., 2023; Shukla et al., 2020). The machine learning results also support previous warnings about validation. The difference between validation and forward holdout performance shows that historical relationships may be less stable in a later period. This is why time-ordered testing and baseline comparisons are important (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012; He et al., 2021).
[Heading 3] 4.10.4 Validation and robustness
[Normal] The results are more credible because future fields were removed from the feature set and the training procedure followed calendar order. However, the findings remain subject to uncertainty. Market coverage may change, rainfall does not capture every agricultural condition, and currency or trade changes may weaken historical relationships. The project therefore provides a structured warning process rather than a guarantee of future accuracy. Predictions should be checked against market availability, household information, rainfall reports, and local knowledge. New observations should be added to the monitoring process so that calibration, drift, and changes in performance can be assessed (World Food Programme, 2025; Zimbabwe Livelihoods Assessment Committee, 2024).
[Heading 2] 4.11 Chapter Summary
[Normal] The summary brings together the evidence on data integration, climate timing, model comparison, feature importance, and decision-support outputs in relation to the project objectives (Mahdi & Bonato, 2015; Westerveld et al., 2021). This chapter presented the results and findings of the food price stress prediction project. The data integration process produced a monthly market–commodity panel linked to districts and rainfall information. The descriptive analysis showed positive-tailed price returns, rainfall variation, class imbalance, and a dual-currency market structure. Rainfall anomalies had their strongest reported negative association with price returns at a two-month lead. The interaction evidence showed higher predicted stress when rainfall deficits and positive price movement occurred together. XGBoost produced the strongest cross-validation ROC-AUC for the broader SPI target, while persistence remained a strong PR-AUC baseline. The tuned histogram-based model recorded the lowest reported Brier loss but weaker forward holdout discrimination.
[Normal] Rainfall anomaly was the most important positive feature in the recorded permutation analysis. Commodity results differed, with stronger XGBoost performance for oils and fats, miscellaneous food, and cereals and tubers. The district risk output identified Lower, Watch, and Higher bands, with several southern and south-eastern districts appearing among the higher-risk locations. The findings support combining climate and market information for early warning, but they also show the importance of careful validation, target clarity, and human interpretation. These findings provide the basis for the conclusion and recommendations in Chapter 5.
[Heading 1] CHAPTER 5: CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
[Heading 2] 5.1 Introduction
[Normal] This chapter concludes the data science project on predicting food price stress in Zimbabwe. It summarises the main findings, evaluates the achievement of the project objectives, explains the implications of the work, identifies limitations, provides recommendations, and proposes areas for future research. The project was developed in response to the difficulty of anticipating food price pressure when rainfall variability, currency conditions, market connectivity, commodity differences, and local geography operate together. The work combined WFP food price records, CHIRPS rainfall estimates, market locations, administrative boundaries, and district information. It then created a forward-looking target and compared machine learning models using time-ordered validation. The conclusions in this chapter are restricted to the evidence reported in Chapter 4. The model predicts a market price stress condition. It does not predict household food insecurity, poverty, malnutrition, or famine directly. This distinction is important because food access also depends on income, assets, production, labour, remittances, and social protection (Mango et al., 2014; Bhalla et al., 2018).
[Heading 2] 5.2 Summary of Findings
[Normal] The project produced two related market stress targets. The primary target identified an unusually large standardised price movement in the following month. The broader Standardised Price Index target identified a price that was high relative to eligible peer markets. Keeping these targets separate was necessary because a price acceleration and a local price divergence do not represent exactly the same market condition. The primary feature panel contained 3,571 clean monthly market–commodity observations and 607 positive stress observations, representing 17.0% of the panel. The broader SPI panel contained 10,459 labelled observations. The final features included current and lagged price returns, rolling price movement, peer-market measures, rainfall anomalies, cumulative rainfall, seasonal variables, commodity group, currency regime, population context, and a climate–price interaction.
[Normal] The first main finding was that the data sources could be integrated into a district-linked monthly panel. Market records were standardised, administrative information was harmonised, and rainfall was joined by district and month. This created a traceable basis for modelling and mapping. Spatial market research supports the need to preserve local differences rather than relying only on a national average (Fackler & Goodwin, 2001; Mahdi & Bonato, 2015). The second finding was a negative relationship between rainfall anomalies and later price returns. The strongest reported association occurred at a two-month lead, with r = -0.22 and a reported significance level below 0.001. The climate–price interaction also showed higher predicted stress when a negative rainfall condition occurred with positive price momentum. This does not mean that rainfall alone determines food prices. It means that rainfall can provide useful information when interpreted together with market conditions, consistent with climate and food market research (Phillips et al., 1998; Shukla et al., 2020).
[Normal] The third finding concerned model performance. XGBoost achieved the highest mean cross-validation ROC-AUC for the broader SPI target at 0.789. Elastic-net logistic regression recorded 0.759, while persistence recorded 0.764. Persistence produced the highest mean PR-AUC at 0.432, showing that recent market conditions were already informative. The Optuna histogram-based model recorded the lowest reported Brier loss at approximately 0.088, but its forward holdout ROC-AUC was 0.419, which remained below 0.500. The cross-validation and holdout results therefore show useful historical discrimination but limited certainty about performance in a later period (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012; He et al., 2021). The fourth finding was that performance varied by commodity. XGBoost performed best for oils and fats with a ROC-AUC of 0.871, followed by miscellaneous food at 0.828 and cereals and tubers at 0.795. Persistence performed slightly better for meat, fish and eggs and for pulses and nuts. This shows that one model should not automatically be treated as equally suitable for every food category because storage, perishability, seasonality, and market integration differ (Gilbert et al., 2017; Porteous, 2019). The fifth finding was that the project produced a prospective scoring structure. The live_prediction.py process checks uploaded data, validates dates, reconstructs the required features, checks peer-market availability, applies the serialised model, and produces a separate output. The map_stress.py process can then support district levelvisualisation. This is a technical prototype rather than a complete national early warning institution, but it moves the work beyond a one-time analysis.

[Heading 3] 5.3.1 Objective 1: Data integration and administrative harmonisation
[Normal] Objective 1 was highly met. The project successfully combined market price records, rainfall information, market coordinates, administrative boundaries, and district context. The data were organised into a monthly panel with market, commodity, currency, date, and district information. This objective was important because the later modelling depended on a reliable geographic and time structure. Without consistent identifiers, rainfall could not be connected to market observations and the resulting district predictions would not be traceable. The project also retained a crosswalk and flagged uncertain assignments instead of hiding them. This supports the reproducibility expected in spatial food market analysis (Fackler & Goodwin, 2001; Mahdi & Bonato, 2015). The objective was highly met at the project level. It does not mean that all possible market data were captured or that geographic uncertainty was eliminated. Those issues are considered in Section 5.5.
[Heading 3] 5.3.2 Objective 2: Feature construction and climate–price analysis
[Normal] Objective 2 was highly met. The project created features representing current and lagged price movements, rolling returns, peer-market differences, rainfall anomalies, cumulative rainfall, seasonality, commodity group, currency regime, population context, and the climate–price interaction. The results also provided evidence that the features were relevant to the project question. Rainfall anomalies showed a negative lead–lag relationship with later price returns, and the interaction analysis showed higher predicted stress under combined dry and positive-price conditions. These results support the use of climate information as one component of a broader market signal. They agree with research showing that rainfall shocks may affect agricultural expectations and prices over time (Phillips et al., 1998; Sazib et al., 2020). The objective was not to prove that the engineered features are causal variables. Feature importance and correlation do not replace an economic identification strategy. The achievement concerns the construction and testing of useful predictive features.
[Heading 3] 5.3.3 Objective 3: Model comparison and time-ordered evaluation
[Normal] Objective 3 was substantially met. The project compared elastic-net logistic regression, XGBoost, histogram-based gradient boosting, persistence, and majority-class baselines. It reported ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, balanced accuracy, Brier loss, holdout ROC-AUC, and confusion-matrix measures. The models were evaluated using time order rather than relying only on random splitting. The objective is classified as substantially rather than highly met because the forward holdout results exposed a generalisation problem. The Optuna model had a holdout ROC-AUC of 0.419, and persistence had 0.220. These values indicate that later-period ranking was weak, even though the average validation results were more favourable. This does not invalidate the comparison. Instead, it shows why later testing is necessary in forecasting work (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012). The conclusion is that the project identified useful model differences but did not establish a permanently reliable best model for every future period. XGBoost was the strongest cross-validation model for the broader SPI target, while persistence remained a necessary benchmark.
[Heading 3] 5.3.4 Objective 4: Prospective scoring and deployment
[Normal] The deployment assessment follows reproducibility principles for data-driven environmental systems (Reichstein et al., 2019). Objective 4 was highly met as a technical prototype. The project produced a repeatable process that accepts new data, checks the input structure, verifies dates, reconstructs features, checks peer-market information, applies a serialised model, and creates a separate prediction file. The mapping process supports district leveloutput. This achievement is important because a model is not useful in practice if it can only be run once by the developer. Separating raw data, transformed data, model files, prediction outputs, and visualisations improves traceability. The use of Git, GitHub, and Streamlit Cloud also provides a practical route for version control and access. The qualification “as a technical prototype” is necessary. The project does not demonstrate long-term institutional ownership, automated live data feeds, a formal model registry, or a completed user-acceptance process. These are recommended areas for future implementation.
[Heading 3] 5.3.5 Objective 5: Commodity and district decision support
[Normal] Objective 5 was substantially met. The project produced commodity-level performance comparisons and divided scored outputs into Lower, Watch, and Higher risk bands. The reported district output identified Beitbridge, Chiredzi, Bikita, and Buhera among locations with higher predicted risk. The objective is not classified as highly met because the district results are decision-support outputs, not validated maps of household vulnerability. They require confirmation using local market information, household assessments, stocks, transport conditions, and reporting coverage. The output is best used to prioritise further checking rather than to allocate assistance automatically (WFP, 2025).
[Heading 2] 5.4 Implications and Impact
[Normal] The implications are considered at theoretical, practical, and educational levels, reflecting the need to connect predictive performance with responsible use (Reichstein et al., 2019; Westerveld et al., 2021).
[Heading 3] 5.4.1 Implications for theory
[Normal] The project contributes to the understanding of food price stress by combining market persistence, spatial comparison, rainfall information, and commodity context. Storage theory suggests that price movements may become sharper when available buffers are weak or future supply is uncertain (Williams & Wright, 1991; Deaton & Laroque, 1992). The project findings are consistent with this general idea because recent price movement contributed to prediction and the persistence baseline remained competitive. The climate results add a second point. Rainfall information did not operate as a complete explanation of price stress, but its importance increased when interpreted with price momentum. This supports a view of food price risk as a compound process rather than a single-variable relationship. A dry period may have a different implication in a stable market than in a market where prices are already rising. The project also contributes methodologically by showing that validation design changes the interpretation of model performance. Average expanding-window results were stronger than the later forward holdout. This supports the argument that machine learning models for environmental and food security applications must be tested under realistic temporal conditions (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012; Reichstein et al., 2019).
[Heading 3] 5.4.2 Implications for practice
[Normal] For practice, the main implication is that a market stress model can support earlier investigation but should not operate as an automatic decision-maker. Government departments, humanitarian agencies, and market-monitoring teams could use the output to identify districts, commodities, or markets that require verification. The output can support questions such as whether a market is experiencing a local price divergence, whether a rainfall deficit is coinciding with rising prices, whether stocks and transport routes remain functional, and whether a possible response should be considered. The model should be reviewed with WFP market monitoring, FEWS NET outlooks, ZimVAC or ZimLAC assessments, rainfall information, and local knowledge. The practical impact will depend on data timeliness and the ability of organizations to act on a warning. A technically accurate forecast that is delivered late or without an agreed review process will have limited value. Operational ownership is therefore as important as model selection (Westerveld et al., 2021).
[Heading 3] 5.4.3 Implications for education and Data Science 5.0
[Normal] The project has educational value because it demonstrates how data science can be applied to a real local problem. It connects data collection, cleaning, geospatial joining, feature engineering, machine learning, evaluation, visualisation, version control, and deployment in one project. For Data Science 5.0, the project also shows the importance of combining technical performance with human responsibility. The model output must be understandable, traceable, and used with domain knowledge. Students and practitioners should learn that a high score does not automatically mean that a system is useful, fair, or safe to deploy. Time order, missing data, spatial uncertainty, data drift, and consequences for affected communities must be considered alongside algorithm choice (Reichstein et al., 2019).
[Heading 2] 5.5 Limitations and Caveats
[Normal] The first limitation is data coverage. WFP market prices do not represent every transaction, farm-gate sale, informal exchange, cross-border movement, or remote market. Markets with more regular reporting may be overrepresented. A missing price record may therefore reflect limited monitoring rather than low market stress. The second limitation is measurement of climate conditions. CHIRPS is useful for rainfall monitoring, but it is not a direct measure of soil moisture, crop condition, irrigation, yield, livestock condition, or household food access. Two districts with similar rainfall totals may experience different agricultural outcomes because of soil, planting dates, crop choice, or access to water (Funk et al., 2015).
[Normal] The third limitation is target validity. The model predicts a defined market price condition. The target does not measure whether households can afford food or whether they are experiencing food insecurity. A market can have high prices while some households remain protected by income or own production, and a household can be food insecure even when the local price signal is not extreme (Tawodzera, 2011; Mango et al., 2014). The fourth limitation is currency and inflation instability. The project includes currency information and standardised price features, but currency changes may alter the relationship between nominal prices and real purchasing power. Exchange-rate changes, parallel-market conditions, and policy changes can create breaks that historical features do not capture fully (Kramarenko et al., 2010; World Bank, 2021). The fifth limitation is temporal generalisation. Cross-validation results were more favourable than some forward holdout results. This indicates that the historical relationship may not remain stable. Climate conditions, trade routes, policy, reporting practices, and market structure can change. The model should therefore not be presented as permanently accurate.
[Normal] The sixth limitation is spatial uncertainty. Market-to-district assignment depends on coordinates, boundary files, and administrative identifiers. A district levelprediction can also hide variation between markets within the same district. The mapped output should be treated as a screening tool, not a complete spatial account of risk. The seventh limitation is feature omission. The main model does not include all variables that may affect food prices, such as fuel prices, transport costs, import restrictions, border delays, stocks, conflict, vegetation condition, soil moisture, temperature, household income, and informal exchange rates. Omitted variables may explain part of the model error. The eighth limitation is limited operational testing. The scoring pipeline was developed and checked technically, but it was not tested over a long period by multiple institutions using live operational data. User interpretation, workflow integration, response capacity, and alert fatigue were outside the scope of the project.
[Normal] The ninth limitation is model interpretation. Permutation importance identifies how the fitted model responds to feature changes in an evaluation sample. It does not establish that rainfall causes price stress or that a negative permutation value proves a variable is unimportant. Correlated variables can share or mask predictive information (Reichstein et al., 2019). The final limitation is threshold and decision uncertainty. Lower, Watch, and Higher bands are useful for communication, but their practical value depends on the cost of false alarms and missed events. Thresholds should be reviewed with users and observed outcomes rather than treated as universal categories.
[Heading 2] 5.6 Recommendations
[Normal] The recommendations are based on the reported performance, the operational prototype, and established food security monitoring practice  .
[Heading 3] 5.6.1 Use the model as a supplementary early warning signal
[Normal] The model should be used alongside existing monitoring systems rather than as a replacement for them. WFP market analysis, FEWS NET outlooks, ZimVAC and ZimLAC assessments, CHIRPS rainfall monitoring, local market reports, and district knowledge should be considered together. A high predicted probability should trigger investigation and coordination. It should not automatically trigger a declaration of food insecurity or a transfer of assistance. This recommendation follows the distinction between market stress and household vulnerability (Mango et al., 2014; WFP, 2025).
[Heading 3] 5.6.2 Establish a forecast-review protocol
[Normal] A documented review process is important because early warning value depends on how forecasts are interpreted and acted upon (Westerveld et al., 2021). An operational team should define who receives the forecast, how often it is reviewed, what evidence must be checked, and which actions are possible at each risk band. Every alert should be recorded as confirmed, rejected, or unresolved. This will create an audit trail and make it possible to evaluate whether the model adds value. The risk thresholds should be reviewed against observed event frequencies, false alarms, missed events, and response capacity. Thresholds should not be changed informally after one disappointing prediction.
[Heading 3] 5.6.3 Check market functioning before selecting assistance
[Normal] Predicted price stress should inform, but not determine, decisions about cash, vouchers, or in-kind assistance. Before expanding cash-based support in a high-risk market, agencies should check trader capacity, market supply, stocks, transport routes, and the risk that additional demand could increase local prices. Where markets are functioning, cash or vouchers may support household choice. Where supply is constrained or prices are accelerating sharply, in-kind support or pre-positioned commodities may be more appropriate. The final decision should use a market function assessment as well as the model probability .
[Heading 3] 5.6.4 Strengthen district and remote-market coverage
[Normal] Coverage improvements are supported by evidence that spatial market integration and reporting intensity vary across locations (Fackler & Goodwin, 2001; Mahdi & Bonato, 2015). Districts that repeatedly appear in the Higher band should receive closer monitoring, not automatic assistance. Additional checks should cover prices, stocks, rainfall, transport costs, market availability, and household conditions. The data system should expand coverage of remote, informal, and small markets. If the data represent urban or formal markets more consistently, the model may understate stress in poorly monitored areas. Improving coverage would improve both accuracy and fairness.
[Heading 3] 5.6.5 Maintain data, model, and deployment controls
[Normal] Versioning and governance are necessary for reproducible predictive systems and for tracing changes in model behaviour . The live_prediction.py process should remain separate from raw historical files. New data should be versioned, validated, and logged. Changes to market identifiers, commodity categories, currencies, district boundaries, or rainfall processing should be recorded because they can change model inputs. The serialised model should not be replaced informally. Retraining should document the data period, target definition, features, validation design, selected model, and approval decision. A model registry or equivalent record should identify which model produced each forecast.
[Heading 3] 5.6.6 Monitor calibration and temporal drift
[Normal] Future use should monitor currency composition, missingness, price distributions, rainfall distributions, commodity coverage, prediction distributions, and realised outcomes. The difference between cross-validation and forward holdout performance makes this particularly important. Calibration should be checked after new outcomes become available. If predicted probabilities no longer correspond to observed event frequencies, the model should be recalibrated or withdrawn until its behaviour is understood (He et al., 2021).
[Heading 2] 5.7 Future Research Directions
[Normal] Future research should first extend the data. Additional variables could include soil moisture, vegetation condition, temperature, crop calendars, fuel prices, transport costs, border conditions, market stocks, imports, exchange rates, and household survey indicators. These variables may help separate climate effects from market and monetary effects. Second, future work should improve the target. The current targets describe market price stress and peer-relative price divergence. Future research could compare these targets with household food-consumption indicators, coping strategies, market-function assessments, and official food security phases. This would show when a market signal translates into household-level welfare effects. Third, the project should be tested using longer rolling-origin evaluation. New data should be added over time and performance should be monitored by season, commodity, district, and currency. This would provide stronger evidence of drift and help identify when retraining is necessary (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012).
[Normal] Fourth, future research could compare additional modelling methods, including calibrated gradient boosting, hierarchical models, probabilistic forecasting, and models that explicitly represent market networks. Any new method should still be compared with persistence and evaluated using the same time-aware design. Fifth, spatial modelling could be improved. Instead of using district level rainfall joined to markets, future work could use market-specific buffers, travel-time distances, road networks, and trade corridors. This may improve the representation of how supply moves between locations . Sixth, future work should conduct user-centred evaluation. District officials, market analysts, humanitarian teams, and food security specialists should test whether the risk bands are understandable, timely, and useful. The evaluation should measure whether the system reduces investigation time, improves targeting of market checks, or adds information beyond existing reports. Finally, deployment research should address governance. A future operational version should have an owner, data-quality responsibilities, access controls, model-version records, an alert-review procedure, and a process for explaining or withdrawing a forecast. These controls are necessary if the system is to move from a student project to a responsible decision support tool.
[Heading 2] 5.8 Conclusion
[Normal] The final conclusion is grounded in the evidence on market integration, climate signals, model validation, and operational deployment  . This project developed and evaluated a data science system for predicting market-level food price stress in Zimbabwe. It integrated WFP food prices, CHIRPS rainfall, administrative geography, commodity information, currency context, and market features into a monthly forward-prediction framework. Three objectives were highly met: the data were integrated into a district-linked panel, the project constructed and examined climate and market features, and a repeatable prospective scoring prototype was developed. Two objectives were substantially met: model comparison produced useful evidence but exposed temporal instability, and commodity and district decision-support outputs were produced but require local validation. 
[Normal] The main substantive conclusion is that rainfall and recent price movement provide useful complementary signals, but neither should be treated as a complete explanation of food price stress. The main modelling conclusion is that XGBoost produced the strongest cross-validation ROC-AUC for the broader SPI target, while persistence remained a strong benchmark and the forward holdout showed that historical performance may not transfer reliably to later periods. The project’s value is therefore not a claim of perfect prediction. Its value is the development of a transparent and updateable process that brings market, climate, spatial, and commodity information together. Used carefully, it can help identify where further investigation is needed. Used without context, it could exaggerate risk, miss poorly monitored areas, or confuse market stress with household food insecurity. The recommended next step is controlled operational testing with new data, existing monitoring organizations, and local users. The system should remain a supplementary early warning tool until its calibration, temporal stability, coverage, and decision value have been demonstrated over time.
